# Testing When Structural Evidence Predicts Drugs

## Scope correction after source recheck on 8 October 2026

The original assessment below overstated how directly affinity-scoring models duplicate the proposed project. RF-Score predicts one endpoint—protein–ligand binding affinity—from multiple descriptors. Its repository also contains variants adding Vina and Cyscore terms, but those variants still predict affinity. RF-Score is therefore a component-level precedent and a possible baseline, not an equivalent implementation of a framework that combines predicted affinity, structural quality, deviation, dynamics, and other evidence to predict several experimental outcomes. The earlier numerical novelty ratings should be treated as provisional and should not be applied to the clarified proposal without a more specific comparison. [RF-Score repository](https://github.com/lachrymator/RF-Score)

Closer partial precedents include GNINA, which reports pose-quality and affinity predictions separately, and DeepRMSD+Vina, which combines a learned estimate of deviation from the native pose with a Vina energy score to optimize docking poses. They establish overlap with integrating structure, energy, and deviation, but do not demonstrate a general framework calibrated to multiple cellular or pharmacological outcomes. [GNINA original paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13321-021-00522-2); [DeepRMSD+Vina published paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36502369/)

Multiparameter optimization provides a different partial precedent. CNS MPO combines six physicochemical desirability scores and evaluates their association with experimentally measured ADME and safety attributes; its six components are equally weighted. A probabilistic MPO approach also exists. These are closer to balancing multiple aspects of compound performance, but neither source establishes the exact proposed combination of affinity, structural quality, deviation, and dynamics with endpoint-specific weights learned from matched experimental observations. [CNS MPO original paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3368654/); [probabilistic MPO original paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4716604/)

The corrected conclusion is that the reviewed methods overlap with individual parts of the proposal. This targeted recheck does not establish either an exact predecessor or absence of one. Novelty depends on the complete architecture, the definition of each deviation metric, the experimentally validated outputs, the fitting of endpoint-specific weights, and demonstrated improvement over relevant baselines. Multiple input factors alone are common; calibrated prediction of multiple distinct biological outcomes is a separate and broader claim.

Deviation must be defined before modeling. RMSD against an experimental pose, variation across docking runs, and displacement during molecular dynamics describe different quantities. Experimental-reference RMSD cannot be supplied at prediction time for a new compound whose bound structure is unknown. Predicted RMSD or run-to-run dispersion can be available, but they require validation and cannot automatically be interpreted as biological stability. Similarly, a docking energy is an affinity proxy, not a measured affinity. The training and deployment inputs must use the same definition.

The remaining report is retained as the initial literature assessment; where its broad novelty verdict conflicts with this correction, this correction takes precedence.

**Candid verdict:** the original proposal is scientifically sensible but not methodologically new. Learning weights that combine docking scores, protein–ligand interaction features, and physicochemical descriptors with experimental labels is already the basis of empirical scoring functions, machine-learning scoring functions, consensus scoring, and QSAR. The project becomes substantially more creative when it stops trying to produce a universal “drug-performance score” and instead asks a harder question: **under leakage-resistant validation, when does pose-dependent structural evidence add predictive information beyond ligand similarity and assay context, and how much of that information survives transfer from ordinary binding to CRBN-mediated degradation?** A conventional protein–ligand prototype is therefore the stronger starting point because it isolates the statistical method from the unusually sparse, heterogeneous, and multistep biology of molecular-glue degradation. It does not validate molecular-glue prediction by itself; the CRBN stage must be retrained and evaluated as a separate transfer problem. The strongest ISEF design is a staged, interpretable **evidence-value and transportability study**, ending with a preregistered prospective PPIL4 test and permission for the model to abstain when a candidate lies outside its domain. On that basis, the current broad concept merits about **5/10 for creativity and 4/10 for significance at ISEF level**, while the refined design merits about **7/10 for creativity and 7/10 for significance if it reaches an external or prospective test**.

## Close precedents already cover the basic weighting idea

The nearest protein–ligand precedent is **ΔVinaRF20**, which uses a random forest to predict a correction to AutoDock Vina from 20 Vina-derived and buried-surface-area descriptors. It is almost a literal implementation of “combine docking and structural scores, calibrate them to experiment,” although it predicts affinity rather than cellular or degradation performance ([ΔVinaRF20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5140681/)). RF-Score had already learned experimental affinity from protein–ligand atom-contact counts, while ECIF later combined extended-connectivity interaction counts with ligand descriptors; ECIF’s fusion of structure and ligand-level information is especially close to the proposed feature architecture ([RF-Score](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/26/9/1169/199938); [ECIF](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/37/10/1376/5998664)). Consensus scoring also predates the proposal: docking outputs have been combined using averages, ranks, voting, mixture models, and learned ensembles, primarily to improve screening enrichment rather than produce calibrated biological outcomes ([Palacio-Rodríguez et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5872818/)). Thus, neither “multiple scores” nor “data-derived weights” can serve as the novelty claim.

The same conclusion holds in targeted protein degradation. DeepPROTACs used PROTAC, target-pocket, and E3-pocket representations to classify degradation from DC50 and Dmax-derived labels ([DeepPROTACs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9681730/)). Later multimodal approaches have fused molecular, protein, three-dimensional, property, and cell-context information; importantly, one published model’s performance fell markedly when evaluated on unseen protein targets, illustrating the difference between interpolation and biological transfer ([Ribes et al., preprint version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02637); [SE(3)-PROTACs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13158127/)). The closest mechanistic analogue is a hybrid deep-learning/quantitative-systems-pharmacology model that combines predicted binding affinities, protein turnover, physicochemical descriptors, and a Hook-effect model to predict PROTAC DC50 and Dmax. Its reported test performance was useful for DC50 but unsuccessful for Dmax, demonstrating why those endpoints should not be collapsed into one score ([deep-learning–QSP model](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13321-026-01152-2)).

These projects also establish an essential conceptual boundary. **Binding-affinity prediction, pose selection, virtual-screening enrichment, degradation, and cellular performance are different tasks.** CASF-2016 explicitly separates scoring, ranking, docking, and screening power; success at one is not proof of success at the others ([CASF-2016](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.jcim.8b00545)). Likewise, molecular glues and PROTACs cannot be treated as interchangeable. PROTACs are bifunctional molecules with a linker connecting target- and E3-binding ligands, whereas molecular glues are typically monovalent compounds that induce or stabilize a new E3–neosubstrate interface. PROTAC data can test general degradation concepts, but cannot directly supply the same geometry, feature definitions, or fixed weights for CRBN glues.

## Originality lies in testing structural value, not inventing a score

The established component is straightforward: train a regression or classifier on docking energies, interaction counts, ligand descriptors, and wet-lab labels. QED and CNS multiparameter optimization further show that weighted desirability functions for molecular properties are already standard ideas ([QED](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3524573/); [CNS MPO](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3368654/)). A manually chosen weighted average would therefore be less novel—and probably less reliable—than existing nonlinear models. It would also suggest that heterogeneous scores have commensurate physical meaning, although docking programs use different scales and require normalization before consensus operations ([consensus-scoring analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9941253/)).

The potentially original contribution is a **failure-aware value-of-evidence experiment**. The model should begin with a strong ligand-only baseline and add feature families one at a time: docking energies, protein–ligand contacts, clashes and strain, buried surface area, receptor-ensemble stability, physicochemical/ADME variables, and assay context. It should then ask whether each addition improves performance on compounds separated not only by random sampling but also by scaffold, publication or assay series, time, and—at the degrader stage—neosubstrate. Destructive controls should randomize poses, substitute an irrelevant receptor, permute docking scores within scaffolds, and remove protein coordinates. If accuracy survives those controls, the apparent “structural” success probably comes from ligand or dataset identity rather than learned molecular recognition. This concern is not hypothetical: ligand-only and receptor–ligand models achieved almost identical performance on DUD-E, revealing benchmark shortcuts rather than interaction physics ([Chen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6701836/)).

The project should produce an **endpoint-specific report card**, not an overall drug score. At minimum, affinity or binding classification belongs in one model; degradation probability, pDC50, and Dmax belong in separate models; and cellular potency, permeability, selectivity, or toxicity should only be added where matched labels exist. A final decision score can combine predicted endpoints only after the desired experimental decision and tradeoffs are declared. Learned coefficients or feature importance are predictive associations, not proof that a feature causes activity. Because correlated descriptors make individual weights unstable, the project should report bootstrap distributions for grouped feature contributions and compare an interpretable elastic-net or additive model with a nonlinear tree-based challenger.

The prospective element raises significance because it tests whether the model changes a real experimental decision. A rigorous negative result would also matter: showing that docking-derived features add no transportable signal after controlling for ligand similarity would be a useful warning against overinterpreting structural scores.

## Molecular-glue-first development would confound method and biology

Public molecular-glue resources are now much larger than they were several years ago, but their headline record counts are **not counts of independent, directly comparable training examples**. MolGlueDB aggregates distinct compounds and entries from hundreds of publications while retaining binding, degradation, biological, structural, proteomic, physicochemical, and PK/PD annotations; those data describe different outcomes under different conditions, not one unified label ([MolGlueDB](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/54/D1/D1510/8239508)). TPDdb likewise contains many PROTAC and molecular-glue records across cell lines and assay types, but its authors identify improved inactive/negative curation as future work ([TPDdb](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/54/D1/D1683/8285777)). After duplicates, close analogues, incompatible endpoints, missing metadata, and same-paper series are grouped, the effective number of independent examples can be far smaller than the database total.

MGD biology adds causal stages that docking cannot observe. A compound must engage CRBN, recruit a neosubstrate, form a productive dynamic complex, orient accessible lysines for ubiquitination, support catalytic turnover, enter and persist in cells, and encounter compatible expression and proteasomal machinery. Even a stable ternary complex is insufficient when lysine presentation or downstream processing is unfavorable, while excessive cooperativity can under some conditions slow turnover rather than monotonically improve degradation ([Roy et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10344917/); [Han, Miah and Zorba](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10125322/)). Measurements are also context dependent: DC50 and Dmax change with cell line, exposure time, concentration range, and assay protocol. Pooling those values as intrinsic molecular properties would train the model on laboratory context as much as chemistry.

For that reason, **a conventional protein–ligand prototype followed by retraining on CRBN glues is scientifically stronger**. The conventional stage supplies a clean test of whether the integration, normalization, uncertainty, and validation machinery works for a defined endpoint such as Kd or Ki. It cannot show that affinity weights transfer to degradation; the MGD stage must explicitly measure how predictive value decays and which new variables—ternary interface, accessible lysines, cell context, and exposure—become necessary. A PROTAC intermediate stage is useful only if resources permit. It offers better-developed degradation datasets and models, but its bifunctional mechanism makes it a methodological bridge rather than a biological substitute for molecular glues.

## Leakage and label quality pose the largest risks

The greatest threat is not the choice of algorithm but **data leakage**. Protein and ligand similarity between training and test sets can inflate apparent performance; after removal of closely related proteins, machine-learning scoring functions have sometimes lost their advantage over conventional scoring functions ([similarity analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28358210/)). LP-PDBbind showed that even chronological splitting does not eliminate related proteins and ligands across subsets, and created similarity-controlled splits plus an independent post-2020 test to obtain a more realistic estimate ([LP-PDBbind](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10462179.1/)). All measurements for the same compound, close scaffold, paper, and assay series must stay within one fold. A multi-target model must additionally group related proteins or binding pockets.

Label heterogeneity creates a second ceiling. Kd, Ki, IC50, cellular EC50, DC50, and Dmax are not interchangeable. IC50 varies with assay design and cannot generally be converted to binding free energy without mechanism and substrate conditions; repeated values for the same system can differ substantially across assays ([LP-PDBbind](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10462179.1/); [BindingDB](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1751547/)). The primary affinity prototype should therefore prefer exact Kd or Ki within a coherent assay regime. Censored degrader observations such as “DC50 > 10 μM” should be modeled as censored data or separate classifications, not converted into fabricated point values.

Three additional risks require explicit controls. First, **all computational features must be regenerated with one locked preparation and docking protocol**; published docking scores from different software, receptor structures, grids, protonation choices, or search depths cannot be averaged. Second, receptor choice and conformational flexibility can change rankings, and blinded D3R challenges show only modest prospective affinity-ranking correlations even for strong methods ([D3R prospective analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5771500/)). Third, uncertainty intervals can fail under distribution shift. The project should report an applicability-domain distance, calibration or interval coverage, and selective-risk curves showing whether abstaining on low-confidence candidates reduces errors; it should never promise calibrated confidence on a new neosubstrate without testing it.

## A four-stage design separates proof from transfer

**Stage 0 should be a preregistered data audit and protocol lock.** Before model building, define one primary endpoint, feature families, grouping rules, metrics, baselines, and success criterion. Count unique compounds, scaffolds, targets, publications, assay contexts, and complete cases separately; do not advertise database rows as sample size. Lock receptor preparation, protonation, docking box, docking seed strategy, exhaustiveness, pose-selection rule, and descriptor versions. A defensible success criterion is statistically supported improvement over the strongest ligand-only baseline on an untouched group-held-out set, with useful calibration—not merely a better result than Vina.

**Stage 1 should validate the method on conventional binding.** HSP90 with a historical D3R-style held-out set is the preferred provisional target because it has a well-characterized pocket, multiple structures, and prospective-style challenge history; exact access and usable label counts should be checked before commitment. CDK2 with carefully curated exact Kd/Ki values is a feasible alternative if HSP90 data access or completeness is inadequate. Train a standardized elastic-net or generalized additive model as the interpretable weighting system and compare it with gradient boosting. Evaluate docking alone, contacts alone, ligand descriptors alone, nearest-neighbor chemical similarity, unweighted/rank consensus, and the full model under nested scaffold plus publication/time splits. Report MAE or RMSE for numerical affinity, Spearman or Kendall correlation for prioritization, and uncertainty coverage. Do not use a broad random PDBbind split as the central evidence.

**Stage 2 should test transportability rather than assume it.** First, apply the same evaluation framework—not necessarily the same fitted coefficients—to a structurally more flexible target or held-out receptor conformations. Then build endpoint-specific CRBN-MGD subsets from curated public data: recruitment or degradation active/inactive, numerical pDC50 where assay metadata and censoring permit, and Dmax separately. Retrain the weights on CRBN examples and add degradation-specific feature families: CRBN engagement, ternary-interface contacts and clashes, ensemble stability, accessible-lysine geometry, predicted permeability or solubility, cell line, exposure time, and assay type. A PROTAC dataset may be inserted as an optional bridge, but results must be labeled PROTAC-specific.

**Stage 3 should quantify performance decay through nested transfer tests.** Report results in the sequence: within-series control, unseen scaffold, unseen publication or assay, unseen neosubstrate, and finally PPIL4. For each step, show the change in discrimination or error, calibration, and abstention rate. This “transfer curve” is more original and informative than a single leaderboard number because it reveals precisely where evidence stops traveling. Deep degrader models already show large drops between random and cold-target or temporal splits, so degradation under harder splits is expected and scientifically meaningful rather than an embarrassment ([SE(3)-PROTACs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13158127/)).

**Stage 4 should reserve PPIL4 for a blinded prospective test.** Before wet-lab results are known, freeze the pipeline and candidate rankings. Select a feasible panel containing predicted high, intermediate, low, structurally diverse, and high-uncertainty compounds rather than testing only top-ranked candidates. Evaluate enrichment of experimentally active degraders, then estimate DC50 and Dmax separately where full dose–response experiments are possible. Compare the proposed model with Vina ranking, the best ligand-only baseline, and a simple consensus. The justified claim would be that the preregistered system enriched PPIL4 degraders within the tested panel or identified unreliable candidates; it would not establish universal drug performance, in-vivo efficacy, or causal mechanisms.

## Conclusion

The strongest project is not “a weighted score that predicts whether a drug works.” It is **a controlled investigation of how much independent information each form of dry-lab evidence contributes, how that information deteriorates under distribution shift, and when the system should refuse to predict**. This framing turns the limitations of docking and structural scoring into the research question itself.

Beginning with conventional protein–ligand binding is therefore the right methodological choice, provided that the project does not present affinity success as validation of degradation prediction. The distinctive scientific arc is proof of the scoring framework, explicit transfer failure analysis, CRBN-specific retraining, and a preregistered PPIL4 experiment. If scope becomes limiting, preserve Stage 1’s rigorous validation and Stage 4’s small prospective comparison; it is better to answer one narrow question credibly than to fit a large retrospective model that confuses data volume with evidence.
